The gale and the static

−84.2 dBm
Sea state
Force 8 gale · 4.5 m swell

At 01:42 UTC the offshore wind sheared against the northern lattice stanchions and the receiver racks answered with their usual sea of hiss. The ocean noise floor had held steady at minus eighty-four decibels since dusk, and there was no shipping clearance on the regional board. The nearest merchant freighter was eighty miles south, sheltering in the Pentland Firth. On 8,412.000 kilohertz the needle lifted by a fraction of a millivolt, and stayed lifted. A dry, microphonic tap echoed through the monitoring cans every 4.302 seconds, exact as clockwork. No callsign prefix, no maritime Morse preamble, no weather-fax synchronising clicks — just a solitary solenoid pulse striking through the Atlantic gale.

I bypassed the exposed whip mast and coupled the input transformer to the sea-ground loop forty fathoms down. The roar of the surface gale cut out in a single stroke, replaced by the profound silence of deep water. Beneath the thermocline the pulse reappeared with startling geometric clarity — no spark-gap discharge, no arcing marine power cable. It was a stabilised carrier centred precisely on 1,200 hertz, keyed with symmetrical sideband ripples. Frequency drift: zero point zero zero hertz per hour. Whatever circuit hums into the sea out there holds a quartz standard calibrated to laboratory precision.

I revolved the twin directional loop antennas through the full compass arc, hunting for the transmission null. At 318.4 degrees north-west the signal vanished into a razor-shadow, fixing the bearing beyond argument. On the light-table, the bathymetric charts along that exact radial show only the Wyville Thomson Ridge: four hundred fathoms of cold abyss, where no cable runs and no seabed survey has anchored in fifty years. The mainland relay at Lerwick checked their spectrum recorders and reported flatline hash. The transmission does not propagate to the coast; it exists only here, within a twenty-mile basin around this platform.

Entry 04 · final

The fading dawn

Transition
Abrupt cessation · 05:08:00
Relief watch

By 05:00 UTC the first cold seam of Atlantic dawn cracked across the eastern horizon. Normally, solar ionisation begins eroding high-frequency transmissions as daylight rises, smearing clear waves into diffuse mush. This carrier suffered no such degradation. Then, at precisely 05:08:00, it stopped — not dissolved, not wavered: severed on the stroke of the minute, as if a hand had lifted from an old copper key fifty miles beneath the swell. The speakers fell back into the empty hiss of sea noise. I signed the paper logbook, folded my headphones onto the receiver chassis, and stepped out onto the wet catwalk. The storm had blown out; the sea was dark, flat, and completely silent.

Some frequencies belong to no vessel.
